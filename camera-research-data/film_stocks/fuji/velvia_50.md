# Fujifilm Velvia 50 — Film Stock Profile

**Type:** Colour Reversal / Slide Film (E-6 process)  
**ISO:** 50 (RVP 50)  
**Format:** 35mm, 120, 4×5, 8×10  
**Production:** 1990–present  
**Significance:** The definitive landscape film. The most saturated colour film ever commercially produced.

---

## Visual Identity

Velvia 50 is polarising — and that is exactly the point. It does not try to render colour as nature made it; it renders colour as the eye *wishes* nature looked. Blues are deeper than reality. Greens have a luminous intensity. Reds glow. The contrast curve is steep enough that shadows go dark quickly, midtones have punch, and highlights clip before they should. None of this is accidental — it is all built into the film's design philosophy.

Velvia was created for nature and landscape photographers who wanted maximum impact from their subjects: coral reefs, mountain sunsets, autumn forests, tropical birds. In this role it is without peer. For anything involving human skin tones, it is generally considered unusable — its reds make caucasian skin orange-red and its contrast makes shadow areas of faces too dark.

The name Velvia comes from "Velvet Media" — the film is designed for rich, velvety colour depth.

---

## Colour Rendering

**Blues:** Extraordinary. Velvia's blues — sky, water, shadow areas — have a saturated depth unlike any other film. Blue skies on Velvia do not look photographic; they look like memory.

**Greens:** Luminous. Velvia's green rendering — particularly for foliage, grass, moss, and botanical subjects — has an almost neon intensity in bright light.

**Reds and oranges:** Vivid and slightly shifted toward magenta. Autumn leaves, sunsets, flowers — rendered with dramatic saturation.

**Shadows:** Deep blue-purple. Velvia's characteristic shadow colour is a deep blue-teal that adds depth and mystery to shadow areas.

**Highlights:** Clip relatively abruptly compared to negative films. Velvia has less latitude than colour negative film — correct exposure is critical. One stop over-exposure can blow highlights that negative film would retain.

---

## Contrast and Tone

Velvia's contrast is steep:
- Toe: Quick — shadows go dark fast
- Midtones: Rich and full — the "meat" of the Velvia palette
- Shoulder: Steep — highlights compress and clip relatively quickly

The overall contrast ratio is approximately 1:320 (compared to 1:120 for moderate-contrast negative films). This produces images with graphic intensity — very deep darks and very bright lights, with rich, saturated colour in the middle tones.

---

## Grain

At ISO 50, Velvia has extremely fine grain — nearly invisible in 35mm and essentially invisible in medium format. The apparent sharpness is very high.

## Use Cases

Landscape photography: primary use. Nature, wildlife, botanical subjects. Anything where colour is the dominant subject. Architectural exteriors in good light. Not for portraits or interiors with people.

---

## Velvia 50 vs Velvia 100

Velvia 100 (introduced 2003) is a more practical version with one extra stop of speed. Colours are still very saturated but slightly less extreme than Velvia 50. Skin tone rendering is slightly more usable. For most purposes where the subject is not a landscape, Velvia 100 is the better choice.
