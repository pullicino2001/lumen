# Kodak Tri-X 400 — Film Stock Profile

**Type:** Black and White Negative (D-76, HC-110, Rodinal, and other developers)  
**ISO:** 400 (commonly pushed to 800, 1600, 3200)  
**Format:** 35mm, 120  
**Production:** 1954–present  
**Significance:** The most important black and white film in the history of photography. The film of photojournalism, street photography, and documentary work.

---

## Visual Identity

Tri-X is not just a film stock — it is a visual language. The body of photographs made on Tri-X constitutes an enormous proportion of the 20th century's documentary record: the civil rights movement, Vietnam, the Kennedy assassination, the moon landing, the fall of the Berlin Wall. When people imagine "news photograph," they often imagine a Tri-X image whether they know it or not.

The Tri-X look is high-contrast, with deep blacks, slightly compressed highlights, and chunky silver-halide grain that becomes more pronounced as exposure decreases or as the film is pushed. It is not a subtle film. It is an authoritative film — images made on Tri-X have a graphic quality, a sense of weight and permanence, that modern ISO 400 digital cameras cannot fully replicate.

---

## Tonal Response

**Blacks:** Deep and solid. Tri-X's shadow rendering is one of its defining characteristics — when shadows go dark on Tri-X, they go genuinely dark. Not lifted grey as on many modern digital monochrome conversions, but dense, dense black. In underexposed areas, detail disappears into rich blackness.

**Shadow zone:** A specific Tri-X quality: the transition between deep shadows and lower midtones has a particular rendering — shadow detail is present but compressed, giving depth and density to shadow regions of the image.

**Midtones:** Full and well-separated. Skin tones, fabric, stone, wood — Tri-X renders all with the classic Zone System quality: each tone distinctly present without blending into adjacent zones.

**Highlights:** Slightly compressed. Bright areas — sky, white shirts, reflective surfaces — roll off gradually before clipping. Better highlight latitude than the reputation for "high contrast" would suggest.

**Overall contrast:** High. This is the most important characteristic to communicate in prompts — Tri-X images have a graphic quality, a visible contrast ratio, that makes them immediately read as "photojournalistic black and white."

---

## Grain Character

Tri-X at ISO 400 has visible, characterful grain. Not the fine, regular grain of T-Max 100 or Pan-F — Tri-X grain is chunky, irregular, organic, and shadow-weighted.

Shadow regions: pronounced grain that adds texture and energy. In very dark shadow areas, grain becomes the dominant texture.

Midtone regions: moderate grain — present but not overwhelming. Adds a filmic quality to skin and midtone textures.

Highlight regions: grain is minimal — bright areas are smooth.

**Pushed Tri-X:**
- ISO 800 (one stop): grain becomes noticeably more prominent, contrast increases. Images have more graphic intensity.
- ISO 1600 (two stops): heavy, chunky grain dominates shadow areas. Very high contrast. Images approach the quality of classic photojournalism in challenging light.
- ISO 3200 (three stops): extreme grain — grain becomes a creative element as much as a recording limitation. Very deep blacks. This is Daido Moriyama territory.

---

## Developer Influence

The developer used to process Tri-X significantly affects the result:

**D-76 (classic):** Medium-fine grain, full midtone range, excellent tonal separation. The default Tri-X result.

**HC-110:** Similar to D-76 but slightly more contrast. Good shadow detail.

**Rodinal (stand development):** High acutance (apparent sharpness), very visible grain, high contrast. The most "grain-forward" Tri-X result.

**Xtol:** Fine grain, extended tonal range, slightly reduced contrast compared to D-76. Portrait-friendly.

For prompt purposes, the D-76 processed result (full tonal range, characteristic grain) is the default Tri-X reference.

## Interaction with Lenses

**With Leica Summicron 35mm or 50mm:** The combination of Summicron's high micro-contrast and Tri-X's graphic tonal rendering produces the archetypal street photograph. This combination defines "photojournalistic aesthetic."

**With Leica Noctilux wide open:** The extreme aperture combined with Tri-X's contrast creates images of intense drama — precise subject in deep available light, heavy grain in shadows, graphic extremity.

**With Nikon FM2 + Nikkor 50mm f/1.4:** The photojournalism setup. Slightly less optical character than Leica but with the same Tri-X tonal authority.
